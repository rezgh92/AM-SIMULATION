\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The order of events in co-firing}

Taken together, the results fix an order of events that any firing programme for this material pair has to respect. Pyrolysis in nitrogen leaves about 2\% of char, relative to the solids, in the glass-ceramic and about 0.5\% in the copper. The char must then be gasified before the glass seals its pores. With the atmosphere held at a fixed fraction of the copper--oxide equilibrium, this requires hours at 770--800~$^\circ$C and is the slowest step of the process. In the model, removing the char also releases the copper. Residual carbon is assumed to retard the sintering of copper (Eq.~\eqref{eq:etacu}), so a fine powder that has been held back by its own char starts to densify as soon as the steam clears it, at 680--780~$^\circ$C in the baseline and well before the glass. This effect has not been measured for photopolymer char, and its strength, $C_{\mathrm{inh}}$, is an estimate. In silver-metallised LTCC fired in air this coupling does not arise, because the binder burns off completely at low temperature and the metal is free to sinter from then on. Here the burnout step is also, whether intended or not, the first sintering step of the conductor. The glass then densifies over a range of about 80~K and stops when it crystallises, and below the strain point of the residual glass the expansion mismatch is taken up by plastic flow of the copper.

This order explains why neither lever works alone. The furnace programme cannot delay the densification of fine copper beyond the burnout, because the burnout has to happen below the coalescence of the glass. The paste cannot be made as late and as steep as the glass (Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}) without losing conductor density. The two designs of Table~\ref{tab:opt} take different approaches. The nominal optimum uses the assumed coupling between char and copper to start both materials together. It reaches the lowest mismatch, but it depends on the timing of char removal and on the strength of the coupling, which are among the least known inputs of the model. The robust design gives up part of that gain and relies on a coarse copper that is intrinsically slow. It tolerates a factor of two in the gasification rate together with 15~K in the glass transition, and it is insensitive to the assumed effect of carbon on copper. Which of the two is preferable depends on what has been measured. Once the gasification kinetics, the viscosity of the glass and the effect of carbon on copper are known much more tightly than these bounds, a design closer to the nominal optimum becomes safe.

\subsection{Relation to tape-based co-firing}

The rule used for the atmosphere, hydrogen tied to steam so that the water-to-hydrogen ratio sits at a fixed fraction of the copper limit, is consistent with the operating point of the IBM process (Fig.~\ref{fig:atm}), and the calibrated glass is consistent with the burnout temperature chosen for it (Table~\ref{tab:ibm}). The model adds no new principle to that process, but it makes it quantitative: how long the burnout must last at a given temperature and steam content, how much carbon is left when the pores close, and how the choice of copper powder interacts with the programme. In LTCC, camber is controlled by adjusting the paste formulation \cite{chang1998effects} or, with master-sintering-curve predictions, the heating schedule \cite{brandt2013improved}. For copper in a crystallising glass, Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder} shows why paste formulation alone is not sufficient. The heating programme sets the order in which the materials are released to sinter, and the paste then adjusts the rate.

Printing changes the problem in three respects. First, a photocurable slurry carries more binder than a cast tape: at 45~vol\% solids the glass-ceramic shrinks by 23\% linearly, against 12.7--15.8\% in the plane of commercial LTCC tapes \cite{dupont951tds,ferroa6mtds}, and it holds correspondingly more char. Second, conductors are no longer confined to printed planes connected by punched vias. The test vehicle contains a spiral, a buried plane and a via defined in one build. In the three-dimensional model the large buried plane appears to control the distortion; tape technology would mesh or perforate such a plane, and a printed layout can divide it freely. Third, printed ceramics may shrink anisotropically between the build direction and the plane, as laminated tapes do: unconstrained IBM laminates fired in H$_2$/H$_2$O shrink about half as much through the thickness as in the plane \cite{dubetsky1982process}. The model treats both materials as isotropic. Anisotropy would change the camber and the shape of the fired part, but not the order of events described above.

\subsection{Limitations}

The results rest on published data and a model; no part has been fired. Four inputs deserve particular caution. The viscosity of the glass was calibrated against statements in patents, not measured, and it describes one glass composition; a different cordierite-type glass will shift the whole window. The gasification kinetics of photopolymer char on either material are unknown and were estimated, so the absolute times of the burnout carry the largest uncertainty of the study (Section~\ref{sec:res_sens}). The retarding effect of carbon on copper sintering is an assumption of the model whose strength was estimated. The viscosity factor of copper was fitted to a coarser powder than those considered here. In addition, the model omits several phenomena that can matter in practice: dissolution of copper oxide in the glass and interdiffusion at the interface, adhesion and delamination, the water released by the glass during crystallisation, the effect of residual carbon on dielectric loss, and cyclic plasticity of the copper in service. The damage indices compare stresses with strength estimates and are meant for ranking designs, not for predicting failure. The three-dimensional runs stop at 98\% density of the glass-ceramic, so the final densification of the copper at the peak temperature, inside an already rigid body, is represented only in the slab model.

\subsection{What to measure first}

The sensitivity analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:sens}) and the one-at-a-time checks of Table~\ref{tab:opt} turn the limitations into a short list of measurements, ordered by their value to the design:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The gasification kinetics of the photopolymer char on each material, in the steam--hydrogen atmosphere of the burnout. Interrupted burnout runs followed by carbon analysis at three temperatures would fix both the rate and its activation energy. No other quantity has as large an effect on the carbon trapped in the glass and on the mismatch.
\item The viscosity of the glass between 750 and 900~$^\circ$C, which fixes $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ and $m$. Beam bending or parallel-plate viscometry of the glass would give it directly, and so would isothermal dilatometry of the printed glass-ceramic, interpreted with the closed form of \ref{app:verification}.
\item The sintering viscosity of the copper slurry, $f_\eta$, from dilatometry of printed copper coupons in dry hydrogen after binder removal.
\item The char yield of both cured slurries, from thermogravimetry in nitrogen.
\item Whether, and how strongly, residual char slows the sintering of the copper slurry, from dilatometry of copper coupons after partial and complete burnout. The nominal optimum depends on it; the robust design does not.
\end{enumerate}
The particle size and solids loading of the glass also matter, but they are specified by the supplier and the slurry formulation. The crystallisation kinetics matter little within the ranges considered, as long as the exotherm peak lies above the sintering range. All of these measurements are routine, and most of them can be made on single-material coupons printed with the same slurries.

\subsection{Implications for three-dimensional packages}

A fired body of this kind would have the properties that made glass-ceramic/copper attractive for high-end packaging. Its thermal expansion, 3.0~ppm\,K$^{-1}$, is close to that of silicon (2.6~ppm\,K$^{-1}$) \cite{knickerbocker2002advanced}, its permittivity is about 5 \cite{herron1980method}, and its conductors are copper. From porosity and filler alone the model predicts 89\% IACS for the conductor of the robust design and 96\% for a paste without filler. IBM reported $2.0\pm0.2$~\textmu$\Omega$\,cm, about 86\% IACS, for copper co-fired in H$_2$/H$_2$O \cite{herron1980method}. The difference points to impurities and grain-boundary oxygen, which the model does not include. Printing adds the geometric freedom that tape lamination lacks. Vertical coils, shielded lines, cavities and ground planes of arbitrary shape can be built into one part. That freedom is relevant to antenna-in-package modules and millimetre-wave front ends \cite{zhang2019antenna,watanabe2021review}, and to substrates for wide-bandgap power devices \cite{chen2017sic}. The present study defines a candidate firing route for such parts and the few quantities that have to be measured before it is tried.
