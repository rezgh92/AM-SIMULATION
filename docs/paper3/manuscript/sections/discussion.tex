\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{The order of events in co-firing}

Taken together, the results fix an order of events that any firing programme for this material pair has to respect. Pyrolysis in nitrogen leaves about 2\% of char, relative to the solids, in the glass-ceramic and about 0.5\% in the copper. The char must then be gasified before the glass seals its pores. With the atmosphere held at a fixed fraction of the copper--oxide equilibrium, this requires hours at 770--800~$^\circ$C and is the slowest step of the process. Removing the char has a second effect, which is easy to overlook: it releases the copper. Residual carbon retards the sintering of copper, so a fine powder that has been held back by its own char starts to densify as soon as the steam clears it, at 680--780~$^\circ$C in the baseline and well before the glass. In silver-metallised LTCC fired in air this coupling does not arise, because the binder burns off completely at low temperature and the metal is free to sinter from then on. Here the burnout step is also, whether intended or not, the first sintering step of the conductor. The glass then densifies over a range of about 80~K and stops when it crystallises, and below the strain point of the residual glass the expansion mismatch is taken up by plastic flow of the copper.

This order explains why neither lever works alone. The furnace programme cannot delay the densification of fine copper beyond the burnout, because the burnout has to happen below the coalescence of the glass. The paste cannot be made as late and as steep as the glass (Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}) without losing conductor density. %% OPT_DISC

\subsection{Relation to tape-based co-firing}

The rule used for the atmosphere, hydrogen tied to steam so that the water-to-hydrogen ratio sits at a fixed fraction of the copper limit, reproduces the operating point of the IBM process (Fig.~\ref{fig:atm}), and the calibrated glass reproduces the reasons given for its burnout temperature (Table~\ref{tab:ibm}). The model does not add a new principle to that process. What it adds is a quantitative account: how long the burnout must last at a given temperature and steam content, how much carbon is left when the pores close, and how the choice of copper powder interacts with the programme. In silver-based LTCC, camber is controlled empirically by adjusting the paste \cite{chang1998effects} or the heating rate \cite{brandt2013improved}. For copper in a crystallising glass the ladder of Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder} shows why paste formulation alone is not sufficient. The heating programme sets the order in which the materials are released to sinter, and the paste only fine-tunes the rate once they are.

Printing changes the problem in three respects. First, a photocurable slurry carries more binder than a cast tape: at 45~vol\% solids the glass-ceramic shrinks by 23\% linearly, against 12.7--15.8\% in the plane of commercial LTCC tapes \cite{dupont951tds,ferroa6mtds}, and it holds correspondingly more char. Second, conductors are no longer confined to printed planes connected by punched vias. The test vehicle contains a spiral, a buried plane and a via defined in one build, and the three-dimensional model shows that their densification and distortion follow from the same constitutive law as in a bilayer. Third, printed ceramics shrink anisotropically between the build direction and the plane, as laminated tapes do: unconstrained IBM laminates fired in H$_2$/H$_2$O shrink about half as much through the thickness as in the plane \cite{dubetsky1982process}. The model treats both materials as isotropic. Anisotropy would change the camber and the shape of the fired part, but not the order of events described above.

\subsection{Limitations}

The results rest on published data and a model; no part has been fired. Three inputs deserve particular caution. The viscosity of the glass was calibrated against statements in patents, not measured, and it describes one glass composition; a different cordierite-type glass will shift the whole window. The gasification kinetics of photopolymer char on either material are unknown and were estimated, so the absolute times of the burnout carry the largest uncertainty of the study (Section~\ref{sec:res_sens}). The viscosity factor of copper was fitted to a coarser powder than those considered here. In addition, the model omits several phenomena that can matter in practice: dissolution of copper oxide in the glass and interdiffusion at the interface, adhesion and delamination, the water released by the glass during crystallisation, the effect of residual carbon on dielectric loss, and cyclic plasticity of the copper in service. The damage indices compare stresses with strength estimates and are meant for ranking designs, not for predicting failure.

\subsection{What to measure first}

The sensitivity analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:sens}) turns the limitations into a short list of measurements, ordered by their value to the design:
\begin{enumerate}
\item The gasification kinetics of the photopolymer char on each material, in the steam--hydrogen atmosphere of the burnout. Interrupted burnout runs followed by carbon analysis at three temperatures would fix both the rate and its activation energy. No other quantity has as large an effect on the carbon trapped in the glass and on the mismatch.
\item The viscosity of the glass between 750 and 900~$^\circ$C, which fixes $T_{\mathrm{g}}$ and $m$. Beam bending or parallel-plate viscometry of the glass would give it directly, and so would isothermal dilatometry of the printed glass-ceramic, interpreted with the closed form of \ref{app:verification}.
\item The sintering viscosity of the copper slurry, $f_\eta$, from dilatometry of printed copper coupons in dry hydrogen after binder removal.
\item The char yield of both cured slurries, from thermogravimetry in nitrogen.
\end{enumerate}
The particle size and solids loading of the glass also matter, but they are specified by the supplier and the slurry formulation. The crystallisation kinetics matter little within the ranges considered, as long as the exotherm peak lies above the sintering range. All of these measurements are routine, and most of them can be made on single-material coupons printed with the same slurries.

\subsection{Implications for three-dimensional packages}

A fired body of this kind has the properties that made glass-ceramic/copper attractive for high-end packaging: a thermal expansion of 3.0~ppm\,K$^{-1}$, close to that of silicon (2.6~ppm\,K$^{-1}$) \cite{knickerbocker2002advanced}, a permittivity of about 5 \cite{herron1980method}, and copper conductors. The model predicts 96\% IACS for the conductor from its porosity alone. IBM reported $2.0\pm0.2~\mu\Omega$\,cm, about 86\% IACS, for copper co-fired in H$_2$/H$_2$O \cite{herron1980method}; the difference points to impurities and grain-boundary oxygen, which the model does not include. Printing adds the geometric freedom that tape lamination lacks. Vertical coils, shielded lines, cavities and ground planes of arbitrary shape can be built into one part, which is relevant to antenna-in-package modules and millimetre-wave front ends \cite{zhang2019antenna,watanabe2021review} and to substrates for wide-bandgap power devices \cite{chen2017sic}. The present study defines a candidate firing route for such parts and the few quantities that have to be measured before it is tried.
