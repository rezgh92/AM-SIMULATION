\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Verification}

The model was checked against closed-form solutions before it was used for design (\ref{app:verification}). Isothermal densification of the glass-ceramic follows the Skorohod--Olevsky solution to within $1.3\times10^{-4}$ in porosity for 4 to 16 cells (Fig.~\ref{fig:verification}a), and the three-dimensional curvature rate of a free copper/glass-ceramic plate converges to the viscous Timoshenko value at least at second order, with an error of 0.05\% at 0.15~mm voxels (Fig.~\ref{fig:verification}b). The analytical camber of Eq.~\eqref{eq:camber} and the finite-element model therefore describe the same mechanics, and the analytical form can be used inside the design search.

\subsection{Atmosphere window for burnout}
\label{sec:res_atm}

Fig.~\ref{fig:atm} places the burnout atmosphere in the plane of temperature and $p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}/p_{\mathrm{H_2}}$. Copper remains metallic below the orange line, which falls from $10^{6.2}$ at 500~$^\circ$C to $10^{4.8}$ at 780~$^\circ$C and $10^{4.1}$ at 1000~$^\circ$C. Carbon gasification by steam is thermodynamically possible above the dashed line, which lies eight or more decades lower. The two constraints therefore leave a wide band of permissible atmospheres, and the choice within it is kinetic. At 780~$^\circ$C, 30\% steam with hydrogen at 20\% of the copper limit ($p_{\mathrm{H_2O}}/p_{\mathrm{H_2}} = 1.2\times10^{4}$) gives a char half-life of 0.43~h. Forming gas (4\% H$_2$) humidified through a bubbler at 20~$^\circ$C, a common choice for copper, contains 2.3\% water; its half-life at the same temperature is 3.1~h, seven times longer, because the steam partial pressure is low and hydrogen inhibits the reaction.

The operating point of the IBM process, H$_2$/H$_2$O $= 10^{-4}$ at $785\pm10$~$^\circ$C \cite{herron1980method}, lies at 17\% of the copper limit and almost coincides with the steam point in Fig.~\ref{fig:atm}. The rule used here, hydrogen tied to steam so that the water-to-hydrogen ratio sits at a fixed fraction of the copper limit, is therefore consistent with the atmosphere used for tape-cast substrates. It also says how the ratio should change if the burnout temperature changes: by a factor of about 2.5 per 100~K, following $K_{\mathrm{Cu}}(T)$.

\subsection{Removing carbon before the glass seals}

The char has to leave the glass before its pores close, and the glass carries far more of it than the copper does. After pyrolysis in nitrogen the glass-ceramic contains 22\,500~ppm carbon relative to its solids, the copper 5\,300~ppm. The difference follows from the densities: both slurries hold a similar volume of binder, but a unit volume of glass-ceramic solids weighs a third as much as the same volume of copper. Carbon on the glass is also the more harmful, since whatever is sealed in reacts with the water released during crystallisation and bloats the part \cite{herron1980method}.

Fig.~\ref{fig:race} maps the carbon left in the glass-ceramic at the moment its pores close, as a function of burnout temperature and hold time. In steam (Fig.~\ref{fig:race}a) the 100~ppm contour runs from 16~h at 710~$^\circ$C to 1.1~h at 830~$^\circ$C, and the window closes abruptly at 850~$^\circ$C, where the glass seals during the hold. The IBM hold of 6~h at 785~$^\circ$C falls in the region with less than 10~ppm. In humidified forming gas (Fig.~\ref{fig:race}b) only the longest hold, 16~h at 790~$^\circ$C, brings the carbon down to 350~ppm; every other combination leaves more than 1000~ppm. Residual carbon also slows the densification of the glass. Acting as rigid inclusions, the 0.03--2\% of carbon retained in forming gas lowers the final density from 0.99 to 0.96--0.99. For this material pair, steam is therefore necessary rather than merely helpful.

\subsection{Two densification ranges}

Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder} compares the free sintering of the two materials after binder removal at 5~K\,min$^{-1}$ in dry 4\% H$_2$. The glass-ceramic shrinks by 23\% between 850 and 933~$^\circ$C (5 and 95\% of its shrinkage) and then stops, because it crystallises: 93\% of the glass has crystallised at 1060~$^\circ$C. Fine copper densifies much earlier, and over a much wider range. A 3~\textmu m powder shrinks between 454 and 702~$^\circ$C; a 1~\textmu m powder between 323 and 463~$^\circ$C. Only for powders of 12~\textmu m or coarser does the midpoint of densification fall inside the range of the glass, and these powders do not reach high density before 1060~$^\circ$C: 83\% for 12~\textmu m and 60\% for 20~\textmu m.

The two curves also differ in shape. The glass densifies over a range of about 80~K, as a Newtonian melt whose viscosity falls steeply with temperature. Copper densifies over 140--350~K, because diffusional creep has a lower activation energy and grain growth slows it as it proceeds. Adding an inert filler to the paste delays and stretches the copper curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:ladder}b): 40\% filler shifts the midpoint of 3~\textmu m copper from 573 to 657~$^\circ$C and widens the 5--95\% range from 248 to 368~K. Neither particle size nor filler, on its own, gives copper a shrinkage curve that is as late and as steep as that of the glass. The matching has to come partly from the furnace programme.

\subsection{Co-firing with an unmatched paste}
\label{sec:res_baseline}

The baseline design combines a 3~\textmu m copper paste without filler with a programme that follows the IBM pattern: pyrolysis in nitrogen to 460~$^\circ$C, a 4~h burnout at 780~$^\circ$C in 30\% steam, and sintering at 960~$^\circ$C for 1~h in dry 4\% H$_2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:programme}a). The design meets the chemical targets. The glass-ceramic retains 33~ppm of carbon when its pores close and reaches 99.1\% density and full crystallinity; the copper is 97.6\% dense, retains 81~ppm of carbon and conducts at 96\% IACS.

It fails the mechanical ones. As soon as steam begins to remove the char, the copper starts to sinter, while the glass is still rigid. About an hour before the end of the burnout hold, the logarithmic shrinkage of the copper is 20.6\% and that of the glass 4.9\%. This is where the free-strain mismatch peaks, at 15.6\%, and where a free bilayer of 0.1~mm copper on 1~mm glass-ceramic would reach a curvature of 58~m$^{-1}$, concave towards the copper. The damage index of an embedded line reaches $\Pi_{\mathrm{Cu}} = 1.6$ at 680~$^\circ$C, during the ramp to the burnout temperature, when the line is pulled in tension by the rigid body around it while its own necks are still weak. The glass then catches up, and its shrinkage overtakes that of the copper at 889~$^\circ$C. The bilayer bends the other way and reaches $-259$~m$^{-1}$ at 930~$^\circ$C. The final logarithmic shrinkages differ by 4\%: 26.3\% for the glass-ceramic against 22.3\% for the copper. For this material pair the difficulty lies less in the final shrinkage than in the order in which the two materials shrink.

\subsection{Copper paste design maps}
\label{sec:res_design}

Fig.~\ref{fig:design} shows how far the materials alone can repair the baseline, with the programme left unchanged. The upper row varies the copper paste. Coarser powder and filler both delay the copper, and the damage index of an embedded line falls below 1 for particle sizes of 4.5~\textmu m and above (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}b). The peak mismatch falls more slowly. Its lowest values, about 9.5\%, are reached at 6~\textmu m with 30\% filler and at 9~\textmu m without filler; coarser pastes are released too late and the mismatch rises again, now with the copper behind the glass. Every step towards a later copper costs density or conductivity (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}c,d). Filler lowers the conductivity directly, by 14 points of IACS for every 10~vol\%, while coarse powder lowers it through porosity: 18~\textmu m copper reaches only 76\% density. Among the pastes that meet the density limit, 9~\textmu m copper without filler gives the best compromise: 9.5\% peak mismatch, a damage index of 0.38 and 91\% IACS. None of them reduces the final camber, which stays between $-190$ and $-260$~m$^{-1}$ for every paste that meets the density limit. The final camber is set by the difference in final shrinkage, which the paste barely changes.

The lower row varies the glass, with the baseline paste. The glass transition decides whether carbon is trapped. Below about 695~$^\circ$C the glass seals during the 780~$^\circ$C burnout and retains about 3000~ppm (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}e). Above that, the carbon stays below 100~ppm, but the higher the transition, the later the glass densifies, and the crystallisation peak has to rise with it if the glass is to reach full density before it crystallises (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}f). The crystallisation peak must not be too high either: above about 1080~$^\circ$C the glass is less than 80\% crystalline after 1~h at 960~$^\circ$C (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}g). The most interesting region is the narrow band around $T_{\mathrm{g}} = 700$~$^\circ$C (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}h). There the glass densifies during the burnout hold, together with the copper, and the peak mismatch falls to 6.5\% while the carbon at closure stays near 50~ppm. A glass chosen to match the copper would thus do more than any paste, but only within a window of about 20~K in $T_{\mathrm{g}}$, and with little margin on carbon at its lower edge.

\subsection{A matched paste and programme}
\label{sec:res_opt}

Neither the paste nor the glass alone brings the peak mismatch below about 6\%, so the paste and the programme were searched together (Section~\ref{sec:numerics}). The best design found within an 18~h cycle, called the nominal optimum below, is listed in Table~\ref{tab:opt} and its history is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:programme}b. It uses a 3.9~\textmu m copper powder with 24~vol\% filler. The burnout is short and hot: 1.3~h at 840~$^\circ$C in 81\% steam, just below the temperature at which the glass would seal during the hold (Fig.~\ref{fig:race}a). A 1.7~h hold at 916~$^\circ$C follows, and then 1.2~h at 1018~$^\circ$C. The peak mismatch falls from 15.6 to 3.7\%, the damage index of an embedded line from 1.6 to 0.83, and the final camber of the reference bilayer from $-254$ to $-76$~m$^{-1}$. The glass-ceramic is 99.0\% dense and fully crystallised, with 0.2~ppm of carbon at pore closure.

The nominal optimum works through the retarding effect of carbon on copper sintering that the model assumes (Eq.~\eqref{eq:etacu}). The copper cannot sinter while it carries char (Section~\ref{sec:res_baseline}), so the burnout releases it. Placing the burnout just below the coalescence of the glass, and making it fast with a high steam fraction, releases the copper at the moment the glass begins to flow. The two materials then densify together (Fig.~\ref{fig:programme}b), and the filler slows the copper just enough to follow the glass rather than lead it. The glass is already 99\% dense when the hold at 916~$^\circ$C begins. The hold crystallises it, while the copper continues to densify, and the peak at 1018~$^\circ$C finishes the copper.

The optimum has two weaknesses. The first is conductivity: 24\% filler limits the copper to 64\% IACS. The second is robustness. The design is sensitive to the timing on which it rests, and the lower part of Table~\ref{tab:opt} shows what happens when uncertain inputs are varied one at a time. If the char gasifies half as fast as assumed, the carbon at closure rises from 0.2 to 76~ppm and the peak mismatch from 3.7 to 11.1\%; at a quarter of the assumed rate the carbon limit is exceeded by an order of magnitude. A glass transition 15~K higher or lower than calibrated raises the peak mismatch to about 10\%. So does any change in the carbon inhibition scale of copper sintering, $C_{\mathrm{inh}}$, by a factor of four or five either way, and at 1000~ppm the damage index of a buried line exceeds 1. A factor of two in the viscosity of copper changes the mismatch less (5.8--6.2\%), but halving it also takes the damage index above 1. A design that relies on the timing of char removal is therefore only as good as the gasification kinetics, the viscosity of the glass and the effect of carbon on copper, and none of them has been measured for this material pair.

The robust search (Section~\ref{sec:numerics}) therefore asked for a design that meets every constraint at two corners of the uncertainty in gasification rate and glass transition and, in addition, keeps the copper above 80\% IACS. The result, listed in the last column of Table~\ref{tab:opt} and shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:programme}c, follows a different strategy. It uses a coarser 7~\textmu m copper with almost no filler and a higher solids loading, and burns out for 2.8~h at 806~$^\circ$C in 83\% steam, some 40~K below the temperature at which the glass would seal during the hold. It then heats quickly to a short peak at 1017~$^\circ$C and needs 16.0~h in all. Both materials start to densify during the burnout hold, the copper once its char has gone and the glass because it is already soft. Being coarse, the copper runs only a few per cent ahead of the glass; the glass overtakes it during the heating to the peak. At nominal inputs the peak mismatch is 9.3\%, the conductivity 89\% IACS and the damage index 0.30. In both search corners the carbon stays below 50~ppm, the damage index below 0.43 and the peak mismatch at or below 11.4\%; the two other corners, not used in the search, give 9.1 and 9.7\% with 34~ppm of carbon at most. Halving the gasification rate or shifting the glass transition by 15~K moves the peak mismatch only between 9.4 and 9.9\%, and the design is equally insensitive to the carbon inhibition of copper and to the hydrogen inhibition of gasification (9.0--9.6\%). Doubling the viscosity of copper is its weak point: the copper density falls to 0.912, just below the limit. The robustness is paid for in mismatch. The nominal value is two and a half times that of the nominal optimum, and the final camber, $-187$~m$^{-1}$, is larger. Most of the remaining mismatch is a difference in final shrinkage, 8.4\%, because the paste now carries more solids than the glass slurry (56 against 45~vol\%), which helps the copper reach density without filler. A gasification rate four times slower than assumed defeats both designs, which then leave 1295 and 940~ppm of carbon in the glass.

\subsection{The printed test vehicle}
\label{sec:res_3d}

The three designs were then fired in three dimensions as the printed test vehicle of Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}a: a $12\times12\times2.4$~mm glass-ceramic block with a buried $9\times9$~mm copper ground plane, a 3.5-turn square spiral with 0.6~mm wide tracks, and a via to a 1.2~mm pad on the upper face. The block is meshed with 12\,800 voxels of 0.3~mm and rests on a setter. The voxel is much coarser than the pixel of the printer, and every conductor is one voxel, 0.3~mm, thick: three times the copper of the reference bilayer, which exaggerates the distortion. Each run starts after binder removal and ends when the glass-ceramic is 98\% dense, at 903--917~$^\circ$C (Fig.~\ref{fig:package}). The setter keeps the lower face flat. The distortion therefore appears on the upper face, which dishes over the buried plane.

The buried plane appears to control the result, although no run without it was made. In the baseline the copper has already densified when the glass begins to flow, so the plane acts as a rigid inclusion. The block shrinks by only 14.9\% in plane, against 23.1\% for the free glass-ceramic, and the glass above the plane, unable to shrink laterally, densifies by shrinking more in thickness. The upper face ends 622~\textmu m lower at the centre than at the rim (Fig.~\ref{fig:package}c), about a third of the fired thickness. In the nominal optimum the copper and the glass densify together. The plane then offers little resistance, the in-plane shrinkage rises to 21.2\%, and the relief of the upper face falls to 150~\textmu m. The robust design lies between them: 18.3\% in-plane shrinkage and 317~\textmu m of relief. The relief follows the peak mismatch of the one-dimensional analysis rather than the final one: the robust design ends with a larger final mismatch than the baseline (8.4 against 4.0\%) but with half its relief. The via and pad raise a small plateau at the centre of every part. That the three designs rank in the same order by relief and by peak mismatch supports the use of the one-dimensional analysis for design. The absolute values, however, depend on the area of copper. A plane that covers 56\% of the part, as here, is the most demanding case, and a real layout would divide it or add dummy metal in the rim.

\subsection{Cooling from the stress-free temperature}
\label{sec:res_cool}

Below about 690~$^\circ$C, the strain point of the calibrated glass, mismatch stresses no longer relax. The difference in expansion between copper (16.5--22~ppm\,K$^{-1}$ over this range) and the glass-ceramic (3~ppm\,K$^{-1}$) must then be carried by the solids. Fig.~\ref{fig:cooling} shows the consequences for a copper plane on 1~mm of glass-ceramic. If copper remained elastic, its mean stress would exceed 900~MPa at room temperature and a 20~mm stack with 100~\textmu m of copper would bow by 250~\textmu m. Annealed copper cannot carry that stress. It yields within about 13~K of cooling and then follows its yield stress, which rises from about 27~MPa at 690~$^\circ$C to 69~MPa at room temperature. The bow falls accordingly, to 13~\textmu m for 100~\textmu m of copper and to 6~\textmu m for 50~\textmu m, and the glass-ceramic stays in mild compression ($-7$~MPa for 100~\textmu m of copper). The result does not depend on the stress-free temperature: starting the analysis at 500, 600, 700 or 800~$^\circ$C gives the same final stress and bow, because the copper yields almost at once in every case. Plastic accommodation makes the thermal-expansion mismatch a secondary problem compared with the sintering mismatch, provided the copper is dense and ductile. The price is cyclic plasticity in the conductor, which matters for thermal cycling in service but is outside the scope of this study.

\subsection{Which inputs decide the outcome}
\label{sec:res_sens}

Fig.~\ref{fig:sens} ranks the fourteen uncertain inputs by their elementary effects around the nominal optimum. Elementary effects are expressed per full range of each factor. The half-life of char gasification dominates: across its range of 0.5--8~h it changes the peak mismatch by 16 percentage points on average and the carbon at glass closure by several orders of magnitude, from well below 1~ppm to above the 100~ppm limit. It also comes second for copper density, because char that is not removed keeps the copper from sintering. The carbon trapped in the glass depends next on the fragility and transition temperature of the glass, an order of magnitude less strongly. The density of the glass-ceramic is governed by its own properties: the transition temperature, the particle size and the crystallisation kinetics. The density and conductivity of the copper depend on the viscosity factor $f_\eta$ and on the filler. The peak mismatch, the quantity that the design sought to minimise, responds to almost every input after the gasification rate: $f_\eta$, the glass transition, the char yield, and the particle size and solids loading of the glass. For every output that matters, $\sigma$ is of the same order as $\mu^*$. The effects are therefore strongly non-linear and interact, which is what a design based on the coincidence of two processes should be expected to show. The tolerances on the copper paste itself (particle size $-25$ to $+33$\%, filler $\pm$5~vol\%) matter for the damage index of a buried line, together with $f_\eta$ and the char yield, and the filler tolerance also for the conductivity. The Avrami exponent and the activation energy of crystallisation matter only for the density of the glass-ceramic, and the crystallised fraction (not shown) remained complete in every run. Three inputs were left out of the screening and checked one at a time instead (Table~\ref{tab:opt}): the carbon inhibition scale of copper sintering, the hydrogen inhibition of gasification and the density at which the glass closes its pores. The last has little effect; the peak mismatch of the nominal optimum stays between 3.5 and 3.9\%. The first two matter as much as the gasification rate for the nominal optimum, and hardly at all for the robust design.
