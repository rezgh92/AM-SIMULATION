- **corrections**:
  - LMM and LCM are conflated throughout the recon, and the distinction is load-bearing. Incus LMM feedstock (e.g. BM-P18) is SOLID at room temperature and liquefies above 50 C, is raked hot, and is routinely SOLVENT-debound in acetone or 'Incusol' — it contains a thermoplastic/wax fraction and does have a genuine melt/wicking branch. Lithoz LCM is a liquid slurry at room temperature. The group's material is a Lithoz slurry on a Lithoz LCM machine, so LMM papers are the right analogue for the copper chemistry and powder, but the wrong analogue for the binder's physical state. Every literature transfer must be tagged with which system it came from.
  - The claim that 'the wicking and capillary-liquid-migration branches are largely inoperative because the cross-linked network does not melt and drain' is overstated to the point of being misleading. Lithoz LCM binder is described in the literature as 'solvents and photoreactive monomers'. Supercritical CO2 extracted 90 wt% of the dissolvable resin fraction from a ceramic/polymer VPP print in 2 h, creating 21 vol% nanoporosity. Resch's TGA of the actual copper photopolymer shows a distinct mass loss at 70-140 C, well before any network scission at 270-430 C. Adding PEG was the single most effective crack-reduction measure in McAleer's neutron study, and adding PPG enabled Si3N4 VPP debinding at up to 3 C/min. There is a substantial mobile, non-network fraction and it is the dominant design lever.
  - The 2.37e-15 m2 permeability anchor is mis-described. It is from Chugunov, Kazak, Amro, Freese & Akhatov, Materials 12 (2019) 3886, 'Towards Creation of Ceramic-Based Low Permeability Reference Standards' — a rock-physics reference-standard paper. The specimen was alumina PRE-SINTERED at 1150 C (necks already formed), at 36.0% porosity, measured with helium at 7.81-7.85 MPa CONFINING pressure and 3.44738 MPa pore pressure. It is not a debound green body at 1 bar. Describing it as 'measured permeability of a debound/pre-sintered alumina body' hides the confining pressure and the pre-sintering, both of which matter.
  - The Knudsen/transition-regime claim (Kn ~ 0.05-0.7, mandatory Bosanquet/dusty-gas) is right for a submicron ceramic but wrong for this material. With d50 ~ 16-18 um and eps_open ~ 0.5 the hydraulic pore radius is ~5 um; at 1 bar and 700 K, Kn ~ 0.03 and the Klinkenberg correction b/P ~ 0.1. Darcy plus a slip correction is adequate at atmospheric pressure. Knudsen physics is only essential (a) at low conversion when pores are still sub-micron, and (b) under vacuum, where at 1 mbar Kn > 10 and the flow is free-molecular. Build the dusty-gas option in, but do not make it the default — it will cost you an order of magnitude in solver time for a 10% effect.
  - The statement that copper's high thermal conductivity makes thermal-gradient stress negligible (dT ~ 0.2 K) rests on a category error. The green and brown body are not bulk copper. Loose 40 um copper powder measures ~0.4 W/(m K) against 412 for solid copper, and a 50 vol% Cu/polymer green body is ~0.8 W/(m K) by a Maxwell estimate — so alpha_eff ~ 3e-7 m2/s, about a third of the assumed 1e-6. Pure conduction lag is still small (~0.7 K for a 10 mm section at 1 K/min, by my calculation), but with an oxidative exotherm the same low k_eff produces tens of K of self-heating. The recon's dismissal of the thermal problem and its simultaneous insistence that Frank-Kamenetskii is 'mandatory' are inconsistent; the second is right and the first is wrong.
  - German's 0.2-25 kPa distortion threshold stress is a SINTERING number, back-calculated from distortion profiles at high homologous temperature (his own text: 'thermal softening lowers the threshold strength, allowing densification'). At 300-500 C copper has no measurable creep. The slumping constraint belongs to the 900-1085 C sintering stage, not to 'Regime IV' of debinding. The recon's Regime IV conflates a strength-of-a-loose-powder problem with a creep problem that occurs 500 C later.
  - The bronze strength trajectory (10 MPa green -> 4 MPa at 400 C -> peak at 600 C) is misapplied. German is explicit that the 400 C dip is ANNEALING — the loss of cold work in a PRESSED compact. A printed LCM body has never been cold-worked, so there is no annealing dip to model. Once the last binder leaves, a printed brown body's strength is set purely by inter-particle friction and van der Waals contact, which for a 16-18 um spherical powder is well under 1 MPa, not 4 MPa. Using 4 MPa as sigma_green in the brown window will make your model dangerously optimistic.
  - The conclusion that 'the cycle must be oxygen-starved or CO2/inert' is contradicted by the best available copper data and should be reversed as a default. Resch debound copper photopolymer parts in AIR at 0.1 C/min with 1 h holds and obtained residual carbon of 0.009-0.020 wt%, against 0.25 wt% after vacuum debinding and 0.38 wt% after argon debinding (all followed by identical H2 sintering) — air is 10-20x better at carbon removal. The 5 wt% residue seen in the argon TGA is exactly that trap. The price of air debinding is Cu2O/CuO formation and +3%/+7.8% dimensional expansion, which H2 sintering reverses. The adiabatic dT of ~5000 K is real, but it is managed by rate control and oxygen starvation of the FLOW, not by abandoning oxygen. Frame it as an oxygen-flux-limited burn, not an inert burn.
  - 'Copper cannot form carbides so residual carbon removal is thermodynamically delicate' inverts the practical situation. Because carbon has no sink in copper it must be gasified rather than dissolved — which is straightforward with an oxidising or CO2/H2O-buffered gas, and the published result is 50-200 ppm C routinely. The genuine thermodynamic subtlety is the opposite one: DRY H2 is a poor carbon remover at sintering temperature (C + 2H2 = CH4 is unfavourable above ~600 C), so a dry-H2-only cycle is the one that traps carbon.
  - The '65% of crack-initiation risk sits below 300 C' figure does appear in PMC11990554 — the exact phrase is 'rapid volatilization below 300 C accounts for 65% of crack initiation risks' — but it is an unattributed assertion in a review, with no experiment or dataset behind it in that text. - **actionable_for_architecture**:
  - Re-rank the physics. The Darcy/permeation solver should NOT be the centre of the system. For a 16-18 um copper powder at 50 vol%, K exceeds the 1e-15 m2 threshold above ~10% open porosity and the predicted safe heating rates run from ~5 K/min at percolation onset to ~500 K/min at full burnout. Build the Darcy solver — it is cheap and it is the analytic check — but allocate the modelling effort to (a) the pre-percolation saturated regime, (b) an oxidation/expansion sub-model, and (c) a mechanics model with an explicit interlayer cohesive zone. Keep a dimensionless-group dashboard (Kn, the ratio of reaction time to permeation time, the Frank-Kamenetskii delta, and P_centre/sigma_green) so the solver can report WHICH constraint is active at every instant rather than assuming one.
  - Add a chemistry the ceramic literature does not have: metal oxidation. The measured debinding-stage dimensional change of a copper photopolymer part is +3.0% diametral and +7.8% in build direction under a 600 C air cycle, driven by Cu -> Cu2O -> CuO. No ceramic debinding model contains this term, and it dominates the dimensional budget. You need a coupled oxidation kinetics model (parabolic/linear Cu2O growth on the particle surface, with Cu2O -> CuO above ~300 C in air), a molar-volume-based swelling strain (Pilling-Bedworth for Cu2O is ~1.68), and an oxygen-transport equation inside the pore network — because the oxidation front is itself diffusion-limited and will be non-uniform through a thick section. This is the most publishable novelty available to you.
  - Make the state vector four-phase, not two. It must carry: crosslinked network (alpha_net), mobile/extractable organic (S_liq, with its own evaporation and low-saturation capillary retention), gas (P, composition), and solid (copper + Cu2O + CuO). The extractable fraction is 20-33 vol% of the organics and it is BOTH the thing that creates the initial pore network AND the primary design lever (PEG/PPG diluents, solvent or scCO2 pre-extraction). Modelling the binder as a single crosslinked phase throws away the lever.
  - Do not model the body as initially 100% saturated. Use V0 in the range 0.03-0.10 as a fitted initial open-pore fraction (the reference VPP simulation uses 0.05). This removes the singular start-up of the Darcy problem and is physically what the 70-140 C volatile loss produces. Expose eps_c and the percolation exponent t as identifiable parameters, not hard-coded constants.
  - Make the failure criterion a MECHANICS criterion with anisotropy, not a pressure cap. Measured green strengths are 20 (X;Z), 17 (Y;Z), 13 (Z;Y) N/mm2 — a 0.65 interlayer knockdown — and are a strong non-linear function of overlap (10 -> 23 MPa as overlap goes 0.75 -> 2). So: transversely isotropic poroelasticity, a Biot coefficient, a separate interlayer cohesive strength, and the residual print stress from over-exposure that Resch identifies as a crack source in its own right. Output the two crack MODES separately (delamination vs translayer) because they are the two labels every experimental dataset uses.
  - Fit kinetics on the FILLED green body in each candidate atmosphere, and fit multi-step. Copper/copper-oxide catalyses and oxidises the binder: the main mass loss shifts from 340-430 C (argon) to 270-500 C (air), and above ~450 C copper oxidation adds MASS, corrupting any naive gravimetric fit. Use a 3-4 pseudo-component DAEM (priors: E0 = 60-105, 130-220, 200-240 kJ/mol; log10 A = 8, 17, 19; mass fractions dominated by the middle component) with an explicit additive oxidation-mass-gain term so the two can be deconvoluted. Multi-rate TGA plus TGA-MS or Py-GC-MS to get the evolved-gas molar mass — do not assume a single M; the reference model uses M = 0.086089 kg/mol (methyl acrylate) and that is a defensible default.
  - Implement a Frank-Kamenetskii constraint as a first-class constraint in the optimiser, not a post-hoc check: delta = (Q rho A E L^2)/(k_eff R T^2) exp(-E/RT) < 0.88 for a slab. It matters because k_eff is ~0.5-1 W/(m K), not 400 — the green/brown body is a thermal INSULATOR even though it is made of copper. Solve the energy equation with a real k_eff(alpha, eps, T) and treat the oxygen flux at the surface, not the furnace setpoint, as the control variable during the oxidative window.
  - Treat the atmosphere as a state, not a boundary label. You need, at minimum, pO2, pH2O/pH2 and pCO/pCO2 as transported fields, because: (i) at 400 C the Cu/Cu2O equilibrium pO2 is ~1e-19 atm, so no practical inert gas prevents oxidation — only a reducing buffer does; (ii) H2 reduces Cu2O with enormous margin (pH2O/pH2 up to ~5e6 at 400 C, ~1.6e4 at 900 C), so wet hydrogen is safe; (iii) dry H2 is a POOR carbon remover above 600 C whereas a trace of H2O or CO2 gasifies carbon above ~700 C at no risk to the copper. The optimiser's decision variables should therefore be (T(t), pO2(t), pH2O/pH2(t)) — a three-channel control problem, which is a much better paper than a one-channel one.
  - Stage the atmosphere and put the hydrogen switch AFTER the oxide is dealt with, or before pores close. Hydrogen disease needs H2 to reach internal Cu2O behind CLOSED porosity. During debinding the body is fully open-porous and steam escapes, so there is no blistering mechanism — the risk window is only the final sintering stage above ~92% density. Encode a hard constraint: complete oxide reduction before the closed-porosity threshold. The published route (pre-reduce powder at 150 C/600 mbar H2/1 h; air debind; H2 reduce and sinter at 1030-1050 C) already respects this.
  - Adopt a proven baseline cycle as the optimiser's warm start and the paper's control: 0.1 C/min in air with 1 h holds at 150, 250, 300, 350, 400, 450, 500 C, terminating at 400 C (the DEL2 variant), then 3 C/min to 1030 C, 4 h under 600 mbar H2. That cycle is 5 days and gives 92% density, <0.001 wt% C and <0.005 wt% O — but only for walls below ~2 mm. Your optimiser's first publishable claim should be a shortened cycle at equal or greater wall thickness, benchmarked against exactly this.
  - Build a control layer, not just a schedule generator. The Lombardo/Retzloff optimum is a continuously increasing heating rate, and the practical realisation is controlled-rate thermal analysis: hold a constant mass-loss (or evolved-gas) rate by varying T. Since you cannot weigh a part in a hydrogen furnace, use an evolved-gas analyser (CO/CO2 concentration) as the observable and close the loop on it — there is patent precedent for exactly this. Architect the code so the same model serves both open-loop schedule synthesis and an online estimator/controller.
  - Include the print parameters as decision variables of the same optimisation, because they are the only levers proven to move the debinding outcome. Layer thickness (20 um debinds 9-11 mm Si3N4 cubes crack-free; 30 and 40 um fail) and overlap (green strength 10 -> 23 MPa from overlap 0.75 to 2) sit on opposite sides of a trade-off with the residual stress that over-exposure creates. The 2M30 offers 10-100 um layers, so this is a genuine design space. An end-to-end print-to-sinter optimiser is a far stronger paper than a furnace-schedule optimiser.
  - Add the pre-extraction branch as a modelled process step. Solvent or scCO2 extraction of the soluble fraction creates 21 vol% porosity in 2 h and sets alpha_c and K0 before any heat is applied; combined solvent + air debinding raised sintered density from ~92% to 95.3% for LMM copper. And keep the ultrafast branch alive: vacuum plus rapid heating with porous graphite felts achieved complete zirconia VPP debinding in under 30 minutes. The optimiser should be able to explore 'pre-extract then debind fast' as well as 'debind slowly', because the literature contains a 200x spread in cycle time and the slow end may simply be unoptimised.
  - Handle vacuum as a genuinely different physical regime, and be sceptical of it. At 1 mbar, Kn > 10 and the transport is free-molecular, so a Darcy model over-predicts the safe rate badly — the recon is right about that. But the empirical evidence cuts both ways: McAleer found vacuum produced MORE cracks than argon at 1 C/min despite lower concentration gradients, while the UFTD work used vacuum to go 40-200x faster. Instrument the model so it can explain both, and do not hard-code an assumption that vacuum is safe.
  - Plan a validation experiment that only your material makes possible. Neutron tomography of the residual-binder profile has been done for ceramic SLA green bodies; copper is a better neutron host than alumina (hydrogen's incoherent cross-section ~80 barn vs copper's ~8 barn total), so a copper replication would give you a spatially resolved, time-resolved validation of the transport model that nobody has. Pair it with interrupted-debinding gravimetry, He pycnometry and transient permeametry at 5-6 conversions to identify K(alpha) and eps_c directly.
  - Uncertainty quantification is not optional here and should be architected in from day one, not bolted on. W_B, d50, K0, eps_c, sigma_green(T), and the binder split are all unknown to within factors of 2-250 (K alone spans 2e-15 to 3e-12 m2 across the plausible d50 range). Build the closed-form beta_crit expression in as the cheap sensitivity kernel, wrap the FE model in a surrogate, and report the OPTIMUM CYCLE AS A DISTRIBUTION with a robustness margin, not as a single curve. That framing is also what makes the work publishable in the absence of a datasheet from Lithoz.
- **still_unknown**:
  - The actual Lithoz copper slurry: binder mass and volume fraction, the reactive/non-reactive split, the copper d50 and PSD, and whether the binder chemistry is closer to a Lithoz LCM ceramic slurry or has been re-formulated for metal. Everything above is inference from the closest published analogues. All of it is measurable in-house in under a week (TGA residue, ash, He pycnometry, laser diffraction, solvent extraction gravimetry, DSC for any melting transition) and should be experiment #1 — the whole model's credibility rests on W_B and d50.
  - Any direct permeability measurement, isotropic or anisotropic, on a vat-photopolymerised green or partially-debound body. The field has none. K_z/K_xy is a pure guess and it is the parameter that decides delamination vs translayer cracking in your model.
  - sigma_t(T, alpha) for any metal-filled photopolymer — the strength as a function of BOTH temperature and binder conversion, and the corresponding interlaminar strength. Room-temperature values and their anisotropy are now known; the hot values are not, anywhere. This is the denominator of your primary failure criterion and it is a clean, publishable measurement (hot 3-point bend on interrupted-debinding coupons).
  - An in-situ strength-versus-temperature curve for a fine copper powder compact — where the zero-strength window actually closes. Dilatometry tells you when it starts to shrink (250-500 C for debound powder; 750 C for a printed part under H2 because oxide reduction masks it) but shrinkage is not strength.
  - The effective thermal conductivity and diffusivity of a copper photopolymer green body, a part-debound body and a brown body. I have bounded it at 0.4-1.5 W/(m K) from loose-powder data and a Maxwell estimate, but there is no laser-flash measurement on this class of material, and the oxide layer will change it.
  - Quantitative catalysis: how much of the 70 C onset shift between argon and air is copper/oxide catalysis of the acrylate and how much is simple oxidative pyrolysis. Nobody has run the control (same resin, same filler, inert vs air, with and without a pre-reduced vs pre-oxidised powder). This is a clean experiment and it determines whether ceramic LCM kinetics can be transferred at all.
  - Any peer-reviewed CO2 or endothermic-oxidant debinding study for copper. The concept exists only in patent literature. My thermodynamic argument says it should work and should be gentle on the copper, but the kinetics of CO2 attack on a crosslinked acrylate at 300-600 C are unknown to me.
  - Whether the Frank-Kamenetskii criterion is actually violated in practice for a 10-20 mm copper section under realistic air flow. My steady self-heating estimate of 25-50 K is based on an assumed peak reaction rate and an assumed k_eff; a single instrumented experiment with a thermocouple at the centre of a thick coupon would settle it, and no such measurement appears in the debinding literature.
  - The heat of reaction per kg of THIS binder under each candidate atmosphere — I used a generic 25 MJ/kg for acrylate combustion. DSC on the filled green body in air, argon, CO2 and N2/H2 would give the real numbers and is a prerequisite for the energy equation.
  - How the copper sediments in a Lithoz LCM vat. Resch identifies slow copper sedimentation producing local resin gradients, hence localised gas evolution and cracks, on a machine with a vertical material reservoir. Copper at 8.96 g/cm3 in a ~1.1 g/cm3 resin is a far worse sedimentation problem than alumina, and the 2M30's vat geometry and inter-layer dwell times will set how bad it is. No published data.
  - Whether the ultrafast (sub-30-minute vacuum + graphite-felt) route transfers to copper at all, and whether the vacuum-causes-more-cracks result of McAleer or the vacuum-enables-speed result of Mosadegh governs. These two results are in direct tension and both are recent and credible.
  - The Si3N4 95%N2/5%H2 mechanism (hydrocarbon formation spreading the gas-evolution pressure) remains unverified quantitatively, and I could not determine whether it transfers to copper — though for copper the H2 is justified on reduction grounds alone, so the question is less pressing than the recon made it.
  - Multi-material co-debinding. The 2M30's whole point is ceramic+metal in one part, and a copper/ceramic interface will have mismatched gas generation, mismatched permeability and mismatched oxidation/expansion (copper EXPANDS during air debinding while the ceramic shrinks). No published treatment of co-debinding a metal-ceramic LCM part exists, and if the group intends multi-material parts this is a larger unknown than anything in the single-material debinding problem.
