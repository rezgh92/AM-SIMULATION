2. Publication and IP terms must be negotiated before the first experiment, not after the results. Using a partner's undisclosed material creates a specific failure mode: you generate results you are contractually unable to describe. Agree now, in writing, a right to publish, a defined review window (30–60 days), what may be redacted, and, ideally, Lithoz co-authorship — a co-author both solves the disclosure problem and makes the paper stronger. Simultaneously decide the framing that survives redaction: the contribution is the *modelling and identification methodology*, with this slurry as a case study, reporting all measured effective properties (kinetic triplet, shrinkage curves, viscous moduli) so the model is reproducible even when the material is not.

3. The central scientific problem is a kinetic race, and a proposal that does not name it is wrong at the core. Residual carbon cannot be removed by carbide formation, so it must be gasified — and carbon gasification (C + H₂O → CO + H₂, C + Cu₂O → CO + 2Cu) is thermodynamically permissive but kinetically negligible below roughly 700 °C, while fine copper begins meaningful neck formation and densification from 600–750 °C. Carbon removal therefore overlaps pore closure, and once porosity closes (around 92–95 % relative density) both the carbon and any reduction-generated H₂O are trapped. Every thermodynamic window must be computed explicitly: the Cu/Cu₂O equilibrium requires pO₂ below about 10⁻⁶ atm at 1000 °C, which means 5.0-grade argon at 1 ppm O₂ sits exactly on the boundary and is not good enough without a getter or an H₂ addition; conversely copper tolerates a very wet hydrogen (pH₂O/pH₂ up to order 10⁴ at 1000 °C is still reducing to Cu), so there is a wide usable window that the proposal should exploit deliberately rather than defaulting to dry H₂. Fix: build a Cu–C–O–H predominance diagram as a design tool, then prove the race is won with interrupted runs — quench coupons at 600, 700, 800, 900, 950, 1000 °C and measure residual carbon, oxygen, and the open/closed porosity split at each point. If carbon is still present when open porosity vanishes, the cycle is unrecoverable regardless of what happens later.

4. Phosphorus and sulfur contamination from the organics is a copper-specific killer that ceramic experience does not flag. Typical LCM photoinitiators are acylphosphine oxides (TPO, BAPO/Irgacure 819) and many dispersants are phosphate esters — both leave phosphorus. The Cu–Cu₃P eutectic is at 714 °C, far below any sintering temperature, so even trace P produces liquid phase, local slumping and embrittlement in the middle of what you thought was solid-state debinding. Sulfur behaves similarly. Fix: ask Lithoz specifically whether the initiator and dispersant are P- or S-bearing, and regardless of the answer run ICP-OES or GDMS for P, S, Si, Na, Cl on sintered coupons. If P is present, it becomes a hard constraint on the entire cycle and possibly a reason to request a reformulated initiator system.

5. Oxygen also sets a melting constraint below copper's melting point. The Cu–Cu₂O eutectic is at 1066 °C and only 0.39 wt% O. Any sintering hold at 1050–1070 °C with incompletely reduced oxide risks incipient melting, not just poor properties. Fix: state a residual-oxygen specification (target sub-500 ppm, ideally sub-200 ppm) that must be met *before* the part is taken above ~1000 °C, and make it a verified gate in the cycle rather than an assumption.

6. The metrology backbone is usually missing. The quantitative state variables for this entire project are residual carbon and residual oxygen, and they are cheap to measure: LECO inert-gas-fusion for O/N and combustion for C/S on green, brown, interrupted and fully sintered samples. Add XRD for Cu/Cu₂O/CuO phase fractions at each stage, Archimedes plus helium pycnometry to separate open from closed porosity (this is how you locate pore closure), and a residual gas analyser or FTIR on the furnace exhaust so you know when debinding actually finishes in the real furnace rather than in a 10 mg TGA pan. Without O and C numbers at every stage, the project is guessing and the paper has no quantitative spine.

7. Debinding is framed as a thermal-kinetics problem when the actual failure mode is pressure-driven fracture. An LCM green body is a crosslinked thermoset filling all space — unlike a MIM part there is no solvent or catalytic pre-debinding step and no pre-existing open pore network, so decomposition gas must generate its own escape path. The governing model is coupled heat transfer + Arrhenius decomposition + Darcy flow through an evolving permeability + internal gas pressure, compared against a temperature-dependent green strength. A model that predicts mass loss but not internal pressure cannot predict the cracking and blistering that will actually destroy the parts. Fix: add permeability evolution (measure on partially debound coupons, or infer from porosity via Kozeny–Carman with a fitted factor) and hot-stage mechanical testing of green and partially debound strength from room temperature through the binder softening range. Also include the reaction enthalpy measured by DSC: in any oxygen-bearing atmosphere binder oxidation is strongly exothermic and a 10 mm section can self-heat by tens of kelvin.

8. TGA-derived kinetics will not identify what the proposal claims. A kinetic triplet fitted at one heating rate is non-unique (kinetic compensation). Required: at least four heating rates (e.g. 1, 2, 5, 10 K/min), model-free isoconversional analysis (Friedman plus KAS/Ozawa) before any model fitting, and blind prediction of a fifth rate not used in the fit. Two further traps specific to this system: copper and Cu₂O catalyse polymer decomposition, so kinetics measured on neat binder are wrong — run TGA on printed, cured green material; and the common trick of burning off binder in air to get solids loading fails outright for copper because the metal gains mass by oxidation while the binder loses it. Determine solids loading instead by inert-atmosphere TGA with a char correction cross-checked against acid digestion plus ICP, or helium pycnometry with geometric density. Finally, TGA gives intrinsic kinetics with no transport limitation; it must never be used to validate the part-scale model.

9. The sintering model as usually proposed is not identifiable from the data usually proposed. Free-shrinkage dilatometry alone cannot separate the bulk and shear viscous moduli from the sintering stress in any SOVS/Olevsky-type continuum formulation — you get a family of parameter sets that all reproduce free shrinkage and all disagree about distortion, which is exactly the quantity you care about. Fix: add loaded experiments — sinter-forging, cyclic or discontinuous loading dilatometry, or at minimum constrained sintering against a setter with known friction — to break the degeneracy. Relatedly, a master sintering curve is invalid here: MSC assumes a single dominant, constant-microstructure mechanism, and you have concurrent oxide reduction, carbon gasification and rapid copper grain growth. If MSC is proposed, it must be tested and reported as a check, not used as the model. Grain growth must be coupled, not ignored, or the late-stage densification prediction will be systematically wrong.

10. Layer-wise printing anisotropy and build-height gradients are ignored by default. Three separate mechanisms produce anisotropic and inhomogeneous shrinkage: interlayer boundaries and cure-depth overlap in the build direction; particle alignment from the recoating/doctor-blade direction if the powder is non-spherical; and sedimentation of copper (8.96 g/cm³) in the vat over a long build, producing a solids-loading gradient from the bottom to the top of the build volume. Fix: measure shrinkage separately in X, Y and Z on every condition (single-axis push-rod dilatometry will hide the effect entirely); measure green density as a function of position in the build plate and build height; ask Lithoz about slurry settling behaviour and the required re-homogenisation procedure. A few percent anisotropy is what destroys dimensional accuracy, and quantifying it properly is itself a publishable result.

11. The printer's own cure profile is a model input, not a constant. Exposure dose sets degree of cure and crosslink density, which sets the pyrolysis kinetics — so the "binder kinetic triplet" is a function of print parameters, and a cycle optimised for one exposure setting may be wrong for another. There is also a gradient of cure through each layer because copper is strongly absorbing and scattering at 460 nm, giving very shallow cure depth. Separately, photopolymerisation shrinkage leaves frozen-in stress that relaxes on first heating through Tg, distorting the part *before* any mass loss occurs — an initial-condition eigenstrain almost always omitted from these models. Fix: photo-DSC and FTIR conversion measurements versus exposure, a TGA sensitivity study across at least two exposure levels, and TMA on green coupons through the softening range.

12. Coupon-scale results are silently assumed to transfer to part scale. They will not: the green body's effective thermal conductivity is of order 1 W/m·K, while sintered copper is around 400 W/m·K, so the Biot number and internal gradients change by two orders of magnitude over the cycle. The radiative boundary condition changes similarly — dark, filled polymer has high emissivity, clean sintered copper has emissivity near 0.03–0.05, so a shiny part late in the cycle absorbs radiant heat far more slowly than the furnace thermocouple implies. Fix: model conductivity and emissivity as functions of conversion and density rather than constants; run an explicit thickness series (1, 3, 5, 10, 20 mm) to locate the critical section thickness at which cracking begins — this is the single most decision-relevant experiment in the campaign; and instrument at least one demonstrator with embedded or surface thermocouples to validate the thermal model directly.

13. Furnace capability is assumed rather than audited, and the assumption is usually wrong. Required and rarely all present: a hydrogen-rated retort or tube furnace with proper interlocks (above 4 % H₂ is flammable); dew-point control hardware — a bubbler/humidifier and a dew-point meter — because you cannot implement a pH₂O/pH₂ window without the ability to set and measure it; a zirconia oxygen sensor on the outlet; 1100 °C capability with better than ±5 °C uniformity across the load, measured with a survey rather than trusted; a tar/cold trap, because condensed binder will otherwise destroy elements and insulation, and binder tar plus hydrogen is a genuine deposit hazard; and preferably a *separate* debinding furnace so the sintering furnace is not contaminated. If vacuum sintering is proposed, reject it: copper's vapour pressure near 1080 °C is around 10⁻⁴ mbar, giving free-surface recession of order micrometres per hour and condensation of copper onto insulation and elements. Use an inert or reducing backfill at a few mbar or above. Also check that any dilatometer to be used is atmosphere-capable and H₂-rated, and note that typical push-rod loads of 20–50 cN will creep a weak brown body and produce false shrinkage — contactless optical dilatometry is preferable for the debound state.

14. Safety and regulatory compliance are usually one line where they need a plan. Hydrogen requires a DSEAR/ATEX-equivalent assessment, gas detection, purge interlocks and a burn-off stack. Binder pyrolysis produces carbon monoxide — odourless and lethal — so CO detection in the furnace room is mandatory, not optional. Uncured slurry contains skin-sensitising acrylates and photoinitiators requiring specific PPE and waste routes. Least anticipated: a brown body of fine copper with high specific surface area and open porosity can self-heat on first exposure to air, so specify either inert transfer or a controlled low-pO₂ passivation step before opening the furnace. None of this is optional for institutional approval and all of it has lead time.

15. Inter-step handling will contaminate your own measurements. Brown bodies and porous sintered copper oxidise in room air within hours; if parts sit on a bench between debinding and sintering, the oxygen content you measure is a handling artefact and your run-to-run scatter will be unexplainable. Fix: define and enforce an inert transfer and storage protocol (argon glovebox or sealed desiccator under argon), record the exposure time for every sample as a logged variable, and run a deliberate exposure-time experiment to quantify the pick-up rate so you know how much slack you have.

16. Setters, supports and the surrounding bed are boundary conditions, not incidentals. Support-removal sites are crack initiators during debinding; setter friction constrains in-plane shrinkage and is a first-order contributor to distortion; a powder bed changes both the gas escape path and the thermal boundary condition versus a flat plate. Copper does not wet alumina well, which is fortunate, but any P- or O-induced liquid phase will stick. Fix: choose and freeze one setter configuration early (alumina plate with BN coating is the sane default), measure the friction coefficient or at least calibrate an effective friction parameter from a constrained-shrinkage test, and treat any change of setter as a change of experimental condition requiring repeat baselines.

17. De-densification and bloating are not in the failure taxonomy but should be. Water vapour generated by late oxide reduction, or CO from late carbon gasification, trapped in closed pores, produces swelling at high temperature — parts that densify and then expand again. This is also the mechanism behind classic copper hydrogen embrittlement, where H₂ reacts with internal Cu₂O to make steam that cannot escape. Both are avoided by the same rule, which should be written into the cycle as an explicit constraint: all oxide reduction and carbon removal must be complete while the part is still open-porous. Fix: include gas generation and trapped-pore pressure in the densification model, and look for the signature in dilatometry (a shrinkage plateau or reversal) rather than smoothing it away.

18. "Optimum heating cycle" is undefined and therefore unachievable as stated. An optimum requires an objective and constraints written as equations: for example, minimise total cycle time subject to peak internal gas pressure below the temperature-dependent green strength, peak internal ΔT below a stated limit, final relative density above target, residual carbon below X ppm, residual oxygen below Y ppm, and distortion within tolerance. Then a stated method — surrogate model plus multi-objective optimisation, or adjoint/model-predictive — and, critically, a feasibility filter against the actual furnace (minimum and maximum ramp rates, gas-switching latency, dew-point settling time, and total hours per run). An "optimum" containing 0.05 K/min segments over 200 hours is not an optimum if you can only run the furnace twice a week. Strongly consider proposing feedback-controlled debinding — ramp rate slaved to the measured evolved-gas flux from the RGA — as the robust alternative to an open-loop cycle derived from an uncertain model. It is more likely to work and it is a better paper.

19. Sensitivity and identifiability analysis is missing and must come *before* the experimental campaign, not after. The coupled model has on the order of fifteen to twenty unknown parameters. Run a global screening (Morris, then Sobol on the survivors) to determine which parameters actually influence the outputs you care about, and use that to design the experiments — a D-optimal design for parameter identification. Then calibrate with a Bayesian scheme (MCMC or similar) that returns posterior distributions, and propagate that uncertainty into the predicted cycle, so the recommendation is "this cycle, with this confidence interval on final density and distortion". Point-estimate fitting of twenty coupled parameters to free-shrinkage data will consume the entire budget and still be non-unique, and reviewers at a good journal will say so.

20. There is no verification step and probably no independent validation. Verification (is the code solving the equations right) and validation (are the equations right) are different things and both are needed: verify with a manufactured solution or an analytical shrinkage benchmark, then validate by blind prediction of a geometry and a thickness not used in calibration. Add a reference-material control: run the whole pipeline first on a well-documented system — a commercial copper MIM feedstock, or an alumina LCM slurry with a published Lithoz cycle — so you can demonstrate the simulation capability works before pointing it at a material whose binder you cannot see. This single addition substantially de-risks both the project and the paper.

21. Sample budget, slurry budget and machine time are not reconciled. The slurry is finite and irreplaceable, and it is the binding constraint on the campaign, not the furnace. Compute grams of slurry per coupon times the number of coupons and check it against the quantity actually delivered, holding a reserve for the final demonstrator and a sealed retained sample. Then reconcile with furnace throughput: a single slow debind-plus-sinter cycle is 40–100 hours, so perhaps two runs per week, and a thirty-run campaign is four months of furnace time. That forces multi-coupon runs, which in turn forces a randomised position map and position as a recorded factor, because furnace uniformity is not perfect. Specify n ≥ 3 replicates for any claimed number; single specimens are not results. Include a day-one baseline run of Lithoz's ceramic-style cycle applied as-is, simply to establish the failure mode you are fixing — it is cheap and it is almost always skipped.

22. Publishability risks are not addressed. The specific threats: no material description means a reproducibility objection at any good journal; novelty is weak if the prior art is ignored — the copper MIM debinding literature has already solved much of the carbon and oxygen problem, and lithography-based metal manufacturing of copper has been published (including by Incus, the metals spin-off of this process family), so "we sintered copper by vat photopolymerisation" is not a contribution; results without uncertainty quantification or independent validation will be rejected on method; and NDA constraints can block disclosure late. Mitigations, all of which should be in the proposal: a formal prior-art review up front that names the differentiator (it must be the predictive model and the identification methodology, not the fact of sintering copper); the method-plus-case-study framing; full deposition of data, model code and identified parameters in a citable repository; the reference-material control from item 20 to demonstrate generality; and agreed Lithoz co-authorship. Finally, add an application-level endpoint — electrical conductivity in %IACS correlated against residual carbon and oxygen — because that correlation is the result a reader actually wants and it converts a process study into a materials paper.

Project mechanics are absent: named owners for each workstream, who writes the constitutive subroutine and in which code (Abaqus UMAT, COMSOL, MOOSE or Ansys — licence availability and the group's actual ability to implement user material models must be stated), who is hydrogen-trained, who runs LECO or where it is outsourced and at what turnaround, and a risk register with explicit go/no-go gates.
